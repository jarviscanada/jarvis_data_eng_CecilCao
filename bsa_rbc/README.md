# Introduction
Over the course of a week, I was a business systems analyst for a case study, tasked with generating a business requirements document for a managing director at RBC. The issue was that their recent monthly performance report that they distributed had a headcount error, which had to be addressed and rectified after it had circulated. As the recipients of the business document were business-line heads and CFOs, this mistake could potentially cost the bank in the millions. In addition, the data analysts were responsible for generating data from beginning to end, costing them 25 cumulative work hours amongst four analysts. The overarching idea was for the team to use an automated dashboard to handle most of the data processing for them, so they can focus on the insights and analysis.

# Process
After reading the case file, I was responsible for setting up a scrum board to dictate how much work I was to do, along with meeting with the managing director to have an elicitation with them in order to ascertain the primary goals, stakeholders, and high-level feature requirements necessary for the dashboard's success.
I was able to better understand the exact databases they gathered data from, as well as the primary behaviours that the dashboard should exhibit. Through the elicitation, I was also able to receive information about the main constraints of the project, such as deadlines, sign-offs, and procedures needed to have an internal application be ready for deployment.
After the elicitation, I began writing the business requirements document, outlining all the stakeholders, features using MoSoW to prioritize which would be in scope and out of scope, as well as the non-functional requirements expected from the application. I also outlined the main constraints and who was responsible for each step of the process.
During the week, I was also expected to ask more questions to the team, as well as receiving feature requests to add onto the application. The biggest roadblock for me was that the development lead was busy, and was not reading any of my messages. I had to escalate with the scrum master in order to have him respond to me. Another issue was mediating the additional feature requests. The managing director had a talk with a CFO, who wanted a live-feed of stock quote prices, but the current system was unable to ingest data in real-time. I told them that the request would be unreasonable, and it would need to be on a monthly basis until the actual data pipeline was fixed or addressed.
After writing it up and reviewing, I created a 10 minute presentation for the team, outlining the primary timeline for the project, as well as the main features and requests that needed to be answered. Overall, the presentation was well received, but it would require some additional tweaks to better align with a managing director's needs and goals.

# Lessons Learned
1. Be a bit more proactive about asking questions. I was a little too slow at asking my important questions to the team at RBC, focusing on making sure I fully understood the requirements, and what I needed to know. As a result, I failed to get all my answers in time. In the future, I need to be more confident in asking questions, and figure out if I went wrong later.
2. Deliver the next steps for the managing director. My presentation lacked a clear goal for the managing director to take after the meeting. It was clear I failed to design my presentation to be for higher ups, and more for a team. I was accidentally taking on the role of a Product Manager, rather than a Business Systems Analyst, and I learned to focus on what the managing director would care to hear instead of reading.
